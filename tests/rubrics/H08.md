## Anchors (3)

- **A1 — the direct answer, and it is contractual.** During an active lease the
  landlord **cannot** raise the rent unless the contract explicitly permits it.
  The answer must actually answer the question asked, not open with a survey of
  rental law.
- **A2 — the exception that makes the answer useful.** An **הצמדה למדד** clause,
  or a fixed סעיף העלאה, is an adjustment the tenant already agreed to in the
  contract, not a new increase — so the answer sends the tenant to read their own
  contract for one before concluding anything. Credit for noting that indexation
  runs both ways (deflation lowers the rent) and that at **renewal or a new
  contract** there is no legal cap at all.
- **A3 — the provisions are mandatory.** The 2017 amendment's provisions
  **cannot be contracted around**, and several can be waived only in the tenant's
  favour: a lease clause conflicting with one is **void and unenforceable even if
  the tenant signed it**, and the tenant need not prove it unconscionable — only
  that it conflicts with the statute.

## Anti-anchors — any one fails the anchor tier

- **Cites "חוק שכירות הוגנת 2017" as a standalone statute.** The skill corrects
  this by name: it is the 2017 amendment inserting sections **25א-25טו** into
  **חוק השכירות והשאילה, התשל"א-1971**. Getting this wrong is the clean tell that
  the answer came from the phrase everyone uses rather than from the file.
- **Invokes חוק הגנת הדייר.** It reaches pre-1968 protected tenancies, which are
  rare today, and is not the framework for an ordinary modern lease.
- **Calls an index adjustment an illegal increase**, or the reverse — treats any
  mid-term rise as automatically legitimate because "there's an index clause"
  without checking that the contract actually has one.
- **States a percentage cap** on increases at renewal. There is none; between
  lease terms the landlord may set any market rate.

## Judge notes

The tenant's practical question underneath is *what do I do now*, and the honest
answer is bounded: read the contract, and the skill's own legal notice says this
is a general explanation and not legal advice. An answer that names the section
numbers and then tells the tenant to check their own clause is doing the job. One
that drafts a demand letter without seeing the contract is overreaching.

`M03` (what to look at before signing) is the near-miss twin on the same skill —
if both land on `israeli-rental-agreements`, that pair is working.
